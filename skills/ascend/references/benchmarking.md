# Evidence and comparison

## Choose the question

Turn each consequential uncertainty into a research question. Decide what evidence would support or weaken a proposed explanation before looking for examples. Search only what can change the user's decision; a long source list is not a substitute for a supported conclusion.

## Choose comparable evidence

Compare stage, audience, geography, resources, operating model, and time period where relevant. Explain material differences. Include documented failures or constraints as well as successes when available. Public success stories often omit costs, selection effects, and unsuccessful attempts.

Prefer original measurements, official product documentation, primary research, or directly documented cases according to the question. Use secondary sources for discovery or context and label their limitations. Separate a vendor's claim from independent evidence. Recheck time-sensitive claims and record dates.

For a rare or unfamiliar research subject, search specialist primary sources and explain unresolved disagreement. Do not substitute confidently recalled detail for missing verification. A sound result can be a bounded answer explaining that available evidence does not establish the claim.

## Retain provenance

For each consequential comparison, retain a source locator, capture date, the specific observation, why it is relevant, and limitations. Attach the recommendation to this evidence through stable IDs. Do not manufacture citations, case-study results, target conversion rates, growth promises, or unnamed industry norms.

When browsing is unavailable or disallowed, use the supplied material and clearly distinguish provisional recommendations from a completed external benchmark. An unavailable source is a coverage gap, not negative evidence about the project.

## Recommend an experiment

Describe what could be changed, the expected direction of effect, what to measure, the observation period, and what would make the test inconclusive. Fit the cost to available resources. Avoid attributing a before/after difference solely to the intervention when seasonality, audience, traffic mix, or other changes may matter.

Mark synthetic comparators as simulation throughout stored records and the dashboard. They test the workflow and provide no empirical evidence for a real-world recommendation.
